Renewable generation is no longer the hard part. Solar and onshore wind are the cheapest new electricity available in most of the world, and the cost curves that got them there show no sign of flattening. The difficulty has migrated downstream — into the wires, the markets and the hours after sunset.

## The grid is the bottleneck

In most mature markets there is now more renewable capacity waiting for an interconnection than there is capacity currently operating. Queues run to years. The projects in them are not blocked by technology or financing; they are blocked by the studies, upgrades and permits required to connect them.

Some of that congestion is being relieved without new transmission at all:

- **Dynamic line rating** measures the actual temperature and wind speed along a conductor rather than assuming a worst-case summer day, typically unlocking 10–30% more capacity on existing lines.
- **Advanced power flow control** pushes current away from congested corridors and onto under-used parallel paths.
- **Grid-forming inverters** supply the synthetic inertia and voltage reference that used to come free with a spinning turbine, allowing far higher instantaneous renewable penetration.
- **Co-located storage** lets a project use a smaller connection than its nameplate capacity, because the battery absorbs the peaks the wire cannot carry.

> Every hour of curtailment is a project that was built correctly and connected badly.

### High-voltage direct current

For distance, HVDC has quietly become the default. Losses are far lower than AC over long runs, the cable is cheaper per kilometre, and modern voltage-source converters can start a dead network rather than merely following one. The links now under construction are what will let an offshore wind farm serve a city several hundred kilometres inland, or let a sunny region export at midday to a cloudy one.

## Storage, at two very different timescales

The industry tends to say "storage" as if it were one product. It is at least two.

### Hours

This is the lithium iron phosphate market, and it is largely solved. Four-hour systems shift the midday solar surplus into the evening peak, provide frequency response on the side, and have become the standard companion to any large PV build. Costs have fallen far enough that the argument is now about interconnection and siting rather than the cells.

### Days and seasons

This is unsolved, and it is where the interesting engineering is. Green hydrogen is the most discussed candidate — electrolysers have fallen sharply in cost, and hydrogen stores indefinitely — but the round-trip efficiency is poor enough that it only makes sense for surplus energy that would otherwise be curtailed. Iron-air batteries, compressed air in salt caverns, and pumped hydro where geography permits are all being built at pilot scale.

## The parts of the system nobody photographs

Two advances deserve more attention than they get.

The first is the **heat pump**, which is not a generation technology at all but does more to decarbonise a building than any panel on its roof. Delivering three to four units of heat per unit of electricity, it converts every improvement in the grid's carbon intensity into an improvement in heating emissions automatically.

The second is **recycling**. The first large generation of modules and turbine blades is reaching end of life, and processes that recover silver, silicon and glass at useful purity are moving from demonstration to commercial operation. A supply chain that reclaims its own materials is a different proposition from one that mines them.

## Where the next five years go

Expect the headline generation numbers to keep climbing and to stop being interesting. The metrics worth watching are curtailment rates, interconnection queue times, and the cost of storage beyond eight hours. Those three numbers, more than any efficiency record, will decide how quickly clean power actually displaces fuel.
