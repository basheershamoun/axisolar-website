For most of the last decade the story of solar was a story about price. Modules got cheaper, then cheaper again, and the industry spent its energy working out how to install them faster. That era has not ended, but it is no longer the interesting part. The gains arriving now come from the cell itself, from the way panels are mounted, and from the software deciding what an array should do minute by minute.

## The cell is finally changing again

Crystalline silicon has carried the industry for forty years and is approaching its practical ceiling. A single-junction silicon cell cannot convert more than roughly 29% of incoming light, and commercial modules already sit close enough to that limit that each additional tenth of a percent costs real money.

Tandem cells sidestep the problem entirely. By stacking a perovskite layer on top of silicon, the two materials divide the spectrum between them: the perovskite harvests the blue and green photons it handles well, and passes the rest down to silicon. Laboratory tandems have cleared 33%, and the first pilot production lines are running.

> The question was never whether tandem cells would work in a laboratory. It was whether they would still be working on a roof in twenty-five years.

Durability, not efficiency, is the remaining obstacle. Perovskites degrade under heat and humidity in ways silicon does not, and a module that loses five percent a year is worthless regardless of how it starts. Encapsulation chemistry is where the serious work is happening.

### What is already shipping

Not every improvement is waiting on a research breakthrough. Several changes are on the market today and are worth specifying on any new build:

- **Bifacial modules** that collect light reflected off the ground behind them, adding 5–15% annual yield on a high-albedo surface such as gravel, concrete or snow.
- **TOPCon and heterojunction cells**, which cut the temperature coefficient so the array loses less output on the hot afternoons when the grid wants it most.
- **Half-cut and shingled layouts**, which reduce resistive losses and keep a shaded row from dragging down its neighbours.
- **Single-axis trackers**, now cheap and reliable enough to earn their keep well outside the desert sites that first adopted them.
- **Module-level power electronics**, which turn a partially shaded roof from a compromise into a design choice.

## Operations became a software problem

A modern plant generates an enormous amount of telemetry, and for years almost all of it was thrown away. String-level current readings, inverter temperatures, irradiance sensors and weather forecasts were each watched in isolation, usually by someone looking at a dashboard once a day.

Machine-learned models now read those streams together. A string whose output has drifted two percent below its neighbours over six weeks is invisible to a human reviewer and obvious to a model. Catching that drift early is the difference between cleaning a connector and replacing a combiner box.

### Forecasting changes the economics

The second software shift is commercial rather than technical. When an operator can predict tomorrow's generation to within a few percent, that output can be sold into day-ahead markets instead of dumped onto the spot market. Paired with a battery, accurate forecasting turns an intermittent asset into something close to a dispatchable one.

## Storage stopped being an accessory

Lithium iron phosphate has taken over the stationary market for unglamorous reasons: it tolerates deep daily cycling, it does not require cobalt, and its thermal behaviour is far more forgiving than the nickel chemistries used in vehicles. Cycle life in the field is now routinely quoted in the thousands, which is what makes daily arbitrage viable.

Longer-duration chemistries — flow batteries, sodium-ion, thermal storage — are being trialled for the eight-to-twelve-hour window where lithium becomes expensive. None has won yet, and the honest answer is that the market will probably support several of them.

## What this means for a project today

The practical advice is unglamorous. Specify bifacial modules where the ground surface justifies them, insist on monitoring granular enough to see individual strings, and size the inverter with the battery you will add in year four rather than the one you are buying now. The cells will keep improving; the racking, the wiring and the data you collect are what you will live with.
