Most articles about going solar are written for people who have already decided. This one is written for the stage before that — when the question is still whether a solar installation makes sense for a particular building, and what it will actually be like to own.

## Start with the roof, not the panels

The single most expensive mistake in residential and commercial solar is mounting a twenty-five year asset on a ten year roof. Replacing the covering underneath an installed array means removing and reinstalling every module, and the labour to do that will comfortably exceed whatever was saved by not re-roofing first.

Before anything else, establish four things:

1. **Remaining roof life.** If it is under ten years, re-roof first. There is no version of this where deferring saves money.
2. **Orientation and pitch.** South-facing is the textbook answer in the northern hemisphere, but east–west splits often suit a household better because they generate at breakfast and in the evening rather than all at noon.
3. **Shading across the year.** A chimney that casts no shadow in June may shade half the array in December. Insist on a survey that models the whole year, not a single visit.
4. **Structural capacity.** Modules are not heavy, but wind uplift on a poorly detailed mount is, and that is what tears arrays off roofs.

> The cheapest kilowatt-hour on any site is the one the building never needed in the first place. Insulation and controls come before generation, every time.

## Sizing is about consumption, not roof area

The instinct is to fill the available space. The better approach is to look at twelve months of half-hourly consumption data, if it exists, and size around what the building actually uses when the sun is up.

Self-consumed electricity is worth the full retail rate. Exported electricity is worth whatever the tariff pays, which in most markets is considerably less. An array that is oversized for its load spends its afternoons selling power at a discount.

### Where a battery earns its place

A battery is worth adding when one of three things is true: the export tariff is poor relative to the import rate, the property is on a time-of-use tariff with a meaningful evening peak, or supply is unreliable and outages are expensive. If none applies, the money is usually better spent on a heat pump or on load-shifting controls.

## Living with an array

Solar is a low-maintenance technology, not a no-maintenance one. The realistic schedule looks like this:

- **Monthly:** glance at the monitoring. You are looking for a string that has fallen behind its neighbours, not for an exact number.
- **Annually:** a visual inspection of mounts, cable management and the inverter's ventilation. Rodent damage to DC cabling is far more common than module failure.
- **Every two to three years:** cleaning, more often in dusty or agricultural settings. In temperate climates with regular rain, the benefit is usually smaller than the cost.
- **Around year twelve:** budget for an inverter replacement. Modules are warranted for twenty-five years; inverters generally are not.

Expect roughly half a percent of output lost per year to degradation. A well-built array in year twenty should still deliver around ninety percent of what it did on day one.

## The honest accounting

A domestic system in a decent location typically pays back in seven to twelve years and runs for twenty-five or more, which makes the remaining years close to free electricity. Commercial installations that consume most of their own output do better, often paying back inside six.

None of that depends on a breakthrough. The technology works, the economics work, and the limiting factor on most buildings is simply the survey nobody has booked yet.
